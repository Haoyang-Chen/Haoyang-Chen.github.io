Editable explanations for reinforcement learning

Editable XRL

Edit an agent’s actions, predictions, and values.

CoChoice connects these parts of a decision in an editable Markov tree. People can reconsider their own expectations, correct the agent’s reasoning, and retain those corrections as it continues learning.

Follow the demonstration Explore the domain probes

Collaboration · Transportation · Medicine · Cognitive modeling

What the study reveals

39-person collaboration study

People could predict the AI, yet still had to work around it. Reading explanations often left people adjusting their own role. Retaining edits during learning supported both understanding and the intended division of work.

01 / Method

Where does the disagreement come from?

A choice depends on the action, the consequences the agent expects, and the value it assigns to those consequences. CoChoice makes all three inspectable and editable.

01 · Behavior

What might it do?

Compare possible actions and their probabilities. Revise the distribution to express a preferred choice.

Behavior edits guide the Actor

02 · Belief

What does it expect?

Inspect predicted consequences, including a partner’s response. Correct an expectation that conflicts with your knowledge.

Belief edits guide the World Model

03 · Benefit

What does it value?

Compare the values assigned to outcomes. Revise an evaluation to express what makes an outcome desirable.

Benefit edits guide the Critic

The tree provides a shared basis for reconsideration and correction. Edits become supervision for the corresponding model components; further reinforcement learning retains that guidance alongside the task objective.

03 / Collaboration study

From accommodating the agent to coordinating with it.

In a randomized, between-subjects study, people collaborated with an AI in Overcooked. Three conditions compared reading explanations, revising them, and retaining edits during continued learning.

04 / Beyond collaboration

Inspecting dispatch, treatment, and attention.

Six stakeholders explored the same action–consequence–value structure in three domain probes. Their corrections show how different kinds of expertise can guide a decision.

Transportation / Ride-hailing repositioning

Will the driver go where the system recommends?

A recommendation’s intended effect on supply–demand balance depends on whether the driver accepts it and arrives in the target region. The probe connects recommendations to the responses that determine their effect.

Behavior
Recommended destinations
Belief
Predicted driver destinations
Benefit
Supply–demand balance and preference satisfaction

A targeted correction

A stakeholder proposed correcting the predicted driver-response distribution when it conflicted with observed behavior, then revisiting the dispatch recommendation using that corrected belief.

What further adaptation needs

Driver preferences depend on context such as traffic and expected income. Stakeholders wanted these changing circumstances and potential hazards to be represented explicitly.

Medicine / Retrospective sepsis-treatment probe

Which predicted changes justify a treatment?

Different recommendations may reflect different expectations about the patient’s response. The probe connects treatment choices to predicted physiological trajectories over 24 hours and estimated mortality risk.

Behavior
Treatment options and their selection probabilities
Belief
24-hour physiological trajectories and their probabilities
Benefit
Estimated mortality risk

A targeted correction

One participant increased the preference for fluids and vasopressor support over observation while retaining forecasts they considered plausible. Another wanted to correct physiological trends without specifying exact values.

What further adaptation needs

Risk estimates need interpretable physiological evidence. Subsequent choices should incorporate the patient’s observed response, with forecasts expressed at a clinically meaningful level of precision.

Cognitive modeling / Hierarchical reading

Which reading decision led attention astray?

A hierarchical RL model decides what to read at the text, sentence, and word levels. The probe exposes how those choices lead to particular fixations and the information the model expects to acquire.

Behavior
Choices at the text, sentence, and word levels
Belief
Information expected along the selected route
Benefit
Controller utility, with higher values preferred

A targeted correction

A participant traced unexpected emphasis on “eco-friendly” to the sentence reader. They proposed raising “skip next word” to about 50%, reducing “read next word,” and revising the associated utility.

What further adaptation needs

A correction at one controller expresses a hypothesis about the reading process. Extending it requires behavioral evidence and cognitive theory about how that change should affect later choices.

Across the three domains

Domain knowledge becomes a correction to a specific part of the decision. Extending that correction to later decisions requires evidence appropriate to the domain—driver responses, observed physiology, or human behavior.

Evidence: six stakeholders, two per domain, in 45–60 minute interviews. These formative probes elicited corrections and design requirements; performance improvements from retraining were not evaluated. Manuscript §7, Figures 8–10.
